The case in 30 seconds

A star that dims always on the same beat

When a planet passes in front of its star, it blocks a little of its light. TESS saw that shadow 85 times in four years, every 11 days or so, always with the same depth and the same duration.

  • 0.2%how much the star dims each time it passes
  • 85transits in four years of TESS data
  • ~2.5×the size of Earth: a mini-Neptune, like K2-18 b
  • 2.4 hhow long each transit lasts, edge to edge
Putting the signal on trial

Seven tests passed. One pending.

The method is to try to prove the signal is an impostor: a binary star, a satellite glitch or a neighboring star that dims. Every test was first calibrated with confirmed planets and known impostors.

Seals

Sealed predictions

When we have something we cannot reveal yet, we store the complete analysis in a file and publish only its SHA-256 fingerprint. The fingerprint cannot be used to rebuild the content, but it changes completely if a single letter is touched, and it is anchored in the Bitcoin blockchain with OpenTimestamps. When we publish the file, anyone will be able to check that we already had it on that date.

A shadow of 0.05%

When a planet passes in front of its star, the star dims a tiny bit, once every orbit. Using public data from the TESS satellite, and without anyone telling us where to look, we found an Earth-sized planet in its star's habitable zone this way. It was already known: it was the test.             Search log

            What we did and what we ruled out

            1. 19 Oct · 14:45

              Round 3 finished: no new planet

              Round 3 is over: 3,457 red dwarfs with data. The program recovered 18 already known planets and candidates, including those of L 98-59 and TOI-270, so the method works. But out of 681 repeating signals and 15,349 single dips, no new candidate came out. The strongest one, a 0.9% dip every 23 hours, turned out to be a double star: it has a second eclipse halfway through the orbit that a planet of that size cannot produce. Another was too large to be a planet, and another changed depth over the years, so the light came from a neighboring star. Nine weak signals remain unconfirmed: no other version of the data sees them. The fake planets we hid mark the limit: we found 22 in 100 of 3 Earth radii and almost none smaller.

            2. 19 Oct · 14:40

              A mistake of ours, fixed

              While testing the filter's rules on the known planets of round 3 we found that one of them threw out real planets when the signal is strong or has many transits: it was ruling out L 98-59 b and c and TOI-270 b. We fixed it, calibrated it on 18 known planets and ran round 2 again with the new rule. No planet had slipped through, and the candidate also passes the corrected rules. Everything is written down in the refutation log, mistakes included.

            3. 18 Oct · 17:20

              Round 3: 3,462 more red dwarfs

              We extended the search to the red dwarfs between 12 and 20 degrees from the south ecliptic pole: 3,462 new stars, with QLP light curves and exactly the same method that found the candidate. In every star we also hide a fake planet to measure what slips through. It is running; when it finishes we will publish the interrogation of every signal.

            4. 18 Oct · 15:00

              Test C: the shadow comes from our star

              We subtracted the TESS images taken before and during each transit, sector by sector, to see which point of the sky the missing light comes from. First we calibrated the method: with a confirmed planet it places the dip on its star, and with a known impostor it places it on the neighbour that gave it away. Then we hid 40 fake signals in the real data: 20 on the neighbour, with the depth it would need to fake everything, and 20 on our star. The method recognises them, and our signal looks like the ones on our star; none of the 20 on the neighbour resembled it. Everything points to a planet, but this is statistical evidence: a transit still has to be watched with a telescope on the ground. This analysis was sealed too, chained to the first one (Bitcoin block 972,040).

            5. 17 Oct · 20:00

              We sealed it before telling

              We stored the complete analysis of the candidate in a file and published only its SHA-256 fingerprint, anchored in the Bitcoin blockchain with OpenTimestamps (block 971,910). We also preregistered it on OSF, under embargo until April 2027, and sent a preliminary report to ExoFOP, where TESS candidates are followed up. When we publish it, anyone will be able to check that we had it that day. From now on every project document carries authorship with ORCID.

            6. 17 Oct · 12:00

              The candidate against the simulations: it looks like a real planet

              We ran the signal through TRICERATOPS, a tool that simulates millions of scenarios (planet, binary, neighbouring star) and works out which one best explains what we measured: three runs of one million simulations. It gives a 7.3% false-positive probability, that is, 93% of being a planet. To know whether that is a lot, we ran the same with a confirmed planet, which gave 7.0%, and with two known impostors, which gave 90.8 and 99.8%. Our candidate looks like the planet. But validation needs less than 1.5%, and with TESS alone not even the confirmed planet gets there. With Gaia we found out who the neighbour is: a background star, much farther away, not a companion. We tried to rule it out from the shape of the transit, but that method failed with a known impostor, so we do not use it. And we fixed a mistake of our own: the transit lasts 2.4 hours, not 1.5 as we had said.

            7. 15 Oct · 23:30

              Second planet round: 943 more red dwarfs, and one signal that holds up

              We went through the 943 red dwarfs on the list that have no 2-minute data, using QLP light curves. These are faint stars: with these data only large planets can be seen. Before starting we found a mistake of our own that was silently dropping whole years of data, and we fixed it. Of 424 periodic signals, 125 had the period of the satellite's orbit: its fingerprint, not planets. The strongest test was looking at the same hours in another independent reduction, TARS: 29 signals vanished there. Of 7,407 single dips none survived; the most convincing one came from a neighboring star, as the pixels showed. One signal is left that passed all our tests and is seen by five different reductions: if it were a planet, it would be about 2.5 Earths across and orbit every 11 days. But 7.7 arcseconds away there is a fainter star that TESS cannot separate, and that could produce it. We are not publishing it yet: first that neighbor has to be ruled out.

            8. 15 Oct · 13:00

              Big planet round: 906 red dwarfs, 19 known planets recovered, no new ones

              We tripled the search with SPOC 2-minute data: 906 red dwarfs, about 22 TESS sectors each, and a single download per star for both searches. The program recovered 19 known planets and candidates on its own, among them TOI-700 b, c and d, LHS 1815 b and the three around TOI-789. None of the remaining 126 periodic signals survived: binaries, echoes of spotted stars that rotate, artifacts that showed up in one reduction and not in another, and signals driven by two or three odd dips. Of 9,315 single dips, 8,281 happened at the same time on other stars; we looked at the last 28 one by one: almost all fell in the trough of a spotted star, and three were the eclipses of a neighboring binary that Gaia had already catalogued. Along the way one of our new tests was rejecting three real planets; we fixed it before using it. With fake planets hidden in the data, the single-dip search finds 45% of 3-Earth planets and 5% of 1.5-Earth ones.

            9. 14 Oct · 21:30

              The last dip under watch was not a planet either

              One dip was left: 0.13% for 9 hours on a nearby red dwarf. This time we did not look at the curve but at the same data processed by others: the star's own pixels every 2 minutes (SPOC), the TARS reduction and our own photometry on the full images. The dip measured 1,546 parts per million in QLP and between −100 and 400 in the others, which is noise. To avoid fooling ourselves, we first ran the same test on two real planets, TOI-1221 b and TOI-813 b: in the SPOC pixels they show up with the right depth. Conclusion: an artifact of a single reduction. We learned that a single dip has to show up in every reduction of the same data, and we added that as a test.

            10. 14 Oct · 18:30

              Planets that passed only once: 4,148 dips, one under watch

              We searched for single transits, from long-period planets that the official pipelines leave out. The search first had to find TOI-4562 b, TOI-813 b (discovered by Planet Hunters TESS volunteers), TOI-712 d and TOI-1221 b as single events. In 300 red dwarfs 4,148 single dips turned up; the strongest test was comparing stars with each other: 3,214 happened at the same time on other stars, from Earth or Moon light in the camera. We checked the last 19 by hand and one is left under watch, too weak to report. To know what this result is worth we hid a fake planet in every star: the search finds 40% of 3-Earth planets and 8% of 1.5-Earth ones.

            11. 14 Oct · 13:00

              Planet finder: it finds TOI-700 d and rules out 14 impostors

              We opened the third module with data from the TESS satellite. Control test: without being told where to look, the program found planets b, c and d of TOI-700, including d, Earth-sized and in its star's habitable zone, from a dip of 478 parts per million. Then it went through 300 red dwarfs near the southern ecliptic pole: 48 distinct signals, 5 already known and no new ones left standing. The most convincing impostor looked like a perfect 2.4-Earth planet, but its dip was eight times deeper when looking at a larger patch of sky: it came from a neighboring star that eclipses. Along the way we fixed five mistakes of our own, among them a test that was rejecting a real planet.

            12. 14 Oct · 09:40

              ZTF round: 44 objects, none new and unnamed; Rubin still down

              We went through the last 30 days of ZTF in the southern sky with a new program that remembers what we already refuted. Of 44 objects, the 14 that passed every test already had a name in the TNS. The prediction for ZTF26abvrskw is on track: its color went from g−r ≈ 0 to +0.3/+0.4 in one week, heading for the +0.8 to +1 we expect if it is a type Ia supernova.

            13. 13 Oct · 15:30

              Orbital anomalies, stage 1: zero anomalies and two outdated orbits

              We opened the second module. Out of 58,163 objects seen by Rubin we analyzed 158 asteroids with known orbits, including a randomly chosen control group. Along the way we found that Rubin's position prediction is approximate, that its clock runs 37 seconds ahead of civil time and that it measures positions with a real precision of 0.025″. With all that calibrated, the control group gave zero false alarms. Two asteroids were left offset by up to 17 times the uncertainty of their orbit. Five and seven other telescopes around the world see them offset the same way: Rubin measures correctly and the catalog orbits are out of date.

            14. 13 Oct · 11:40

              Round 5: a trial run with Rubin in the deep south

              We built the search for Rubin alerts in the region south of −30° that ZTF cannot reach. Rubin has been off the sky since the 15 July storm, so we tested it on the 47,157 alerts from 1 to 14 July stored by the Fink broker. We fell into three traps and fixed them: sources that were fading, variables Rubin had already seen before, and fixed sources that looked new because the field was being observed for the first time that night. Five real transients were left: two already had a name in the TNS and three were never reported, three months later. We are not publishing their positions until we decide whether to report them.

            15. 13 Oct · 10:30

              Round 4b: the survivors already had names

              We cross-matched the three objects from round 4 with the Transient Name Server. All three had already been reported by other teams: ZTF26abvrskw is AT 2026acmr, ZTF26abyuhcm is AT 2026aesh (both from ZTF) and ZTF26abwgnzm is AT 2026abzy, discovered by GOTO. None is classified yet. This sample ends with no new discoveries, which is what to expect: professional teams report almost everything in the ZTF alerts within a day or two. To be first you have to look earlier, or look where ZTF cannot reach.

            16. 13 Oct · 09:50

              Round 4: the best candidate was a type Ia supernova

              We got the distance to the three host galaxies from Legacy Survey photometric redshifts: z ≈ 0.13, 0.15 and 0.18. With that we fitted the standard type Ia supernova model (SALT3). ZTF26abvrskw fits almost perfectly, with normal shape, color and brightness, while a typical tidal disruption model fits much worse. It is refuted as unusual. We wrote down a prediction that would test it again: in two or three weeks its color should redden to g−r ≈ +0.9. Of the two still standing, ZTF26abwgnzm rises more slowly than a normal Ia: it is the one most worth following.

            17. 13 Oct · 00:45

              Round 3b: no reliable period or transit

              We searched for periodic variations and dips in the 2018–2025 history of the five stellar sources. We fell twice into calendar traps: ZTF observes once per night, and that creates fake periods of half a day, one day or two days. After excluding them, three stars show signals stronger than the noise, but each has “twins” of equal strength one day apart and there is no way to tell which one is real. No dip passed as a transit. The image cutouts of the three candidates show clean point sources.

            18. 13 Oct · 00:13

              Round 3: 3 candidates left out of 8

              We downloaded the ALeRCE light curves and the history of each spot in the ZTF 2018–2025 archive, and cross-matched with Gaia. Gaia found five sources that SIMBAD did not have: two stars that had actually faded, a variable that switches on and off, a likely quasar and a source that was already varying. ZTF26abvrskw shows no signs of an active nucleus, but its color is not the typical blue of a tidal disruption event either. A supernova near the nucleus is still not ruled out.

            19. 12 Oct · 23:57

              Round 2: 8 of 13 candidates still standing

              Three tests per object: the SIMBAD catalog, age and number of detections. Four were variables seen since 2018–2024. Another turned out to be a known BL Lac at z = 0.304. Matching a galaxy does not refute a transient, so ZTF26abvrskw stayed as a candidate.

            20. 12 Oct · 23:54

              We fixed the criterion: what doesn't fit is what we're looking for

              The initial filter was discarding objects with weak classifications. But what fits no class is exactly the goal. The 12 discarded objects went back on the list.

            21. 12 Oct · 23:48

              First result refuted: it was our own mistake

              All six classes returned exactly 177 objects. A coincidence like that is not astronomy: the class filter was not being applied because of a misnamed parameter. Fixed; that whole result was thrown out.

            22. 12 Oct · 23:41

              The search begins

              New objects from the last 30 days with negative declination, in six uncommon classes: superluminous supernovae, tidal disruption events, kilonovae, novae, blazars and young stars.

            About the project

            An independent search, built on open data

            Cielo Austral looks for unusual things in the southern sky (supernovae, orbits that do not fit and planets) using public data from ZTF, Rubin, Gaia and TESS. The method never changes: try to prove that each signal is not new, and publish every rejection, mistakes included.

            Data and credits

            We work with open data

            ALeRCE

            Chilean broker that classifies ZTF and Rubin alerts in real time. Our light curves, the 2018–2025 history and the image cutouts come from it.

            Zwicky Transient Facility

            Survey from Palomar Observatory, with 1″ pixels. It reaches down to about −30° declination.

            Positions, brightness, parallax and variability classification for almost 2 billion sources.

            CDS · SIMBAD y VizieR

            SIMBAD, AllWISE, Milliquas, eROSITA's eRASS1 and the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (BSC5) that draws the observatory sky.

            Vera C. Rubin Observatory

            From Cerro Pachón, Chile. Public alerts since 24 February 2026.

            Transient Name Server

            The official IAU registry where transients are reported and named.

            TESS (NASA) · SPOC y QLP

            Light curves from the TESS satellite via MAST: SPOC at 2-minute cadence and the Quick-Look Pipeline (MIT) from the full-frame images.

            ExoFOP · NASA Exoplanet Archive

            Official (TOI) and community (CTOI) TESS candidates and confirmed planets, to know what is already known. Eclipsing binaries: Prša et al. 2022.
